How the EQUITAS redesign works

A step by step walkthrough of the problem, the idea, and what a person goes through from landing on the site to getting real help.

The short version

EQUITAS teaches researchers how their grant applications will be judged, so they can fix them before sending. The tools are good. The problem is that nobody can find the right one. We are fixing that by organising the site around the research journey instead of a list of tools.

1

The problem

The site shows twelve tools in one big grid. Everyone sees the same twelve, in the same order, whoever they are.

A new person lands and cannot tell which one is for them. The names do not help. One tool is described as “PRAM, RPPR, NCE”. Those are search keywords, not an explanation. If you already know what they mean, you did not need the help.

The founder put it well in her own answers: a new user does not know what they do not know.

Now

Twelve tools, flat grid, same for everybody. A first-time person has to already know what they want.

After

Pick where you are in your research. You see the three or four tools that fit, and nothing else.

2

The idea

Instead of asking “which tool do you want?”, the site asks “where are you in your research?”

Anyone can answer that. You may never have heard of a Study Section Simulator, but you know whether you are writing a grant or waiting on a result.

3

Who uses it

Four different kinds of people, wanting different things from the same twelve tools. This is why one grid for everyone does not work.

PersonWhat they wantDo they pay?
TraineeHelp with a first grant, with nobody to askNo, free
InvestigatorTo know the score before sending, or why the last one failedSometimes
Grants managerTo track awards, money and paperwork across many peopleThrough the university
UniversityBetter results across all their researchersYes. This is the money

The homepage speaks only to the first two. The other two get their own route, so the two messages never get mixed.

4

Things she needs to decide

Four questions change how the site is built. We have picked an answer for each so work can continue, but these are hers to confirm.

Where do the money and paperwork tools go?

Our answer: add a sixth stage called Manage. Her five stages left three working tools homeless, and left a gap between winning a grant and finishing the project.

Is a grants manager a separate audience?

Our answer: yes, but under the university section. They use the product daily, so they need real pages, not just a link.

What about Transform, where nothing is built?

Our answer: keep it, label it Coming, and ask people what would help. Removing it breaks the story. Hiding that it is empty breaks the honesty.

Who rewrites the tool descriptions?

Our answer: rewrite all twelve as one plain sentence each. This is a day of writing and it changes how the whole product reads.

5

What a new person goes through

This is the main path. A young researcher with a deadline and nobody to ask.

  1. They land on the homepageThe first line names their problem, not the product: most researchers were never shown how grant review works.
  2. They find their stageSix stages laid out in order. They recognise themselves in one and click it.
  3. They see only what fitsThree or four tools, each with a plain sentence saying what it does. Not twelve.
  4. They make a free accountNo card, no sales call. The value is not locked away.
  5. They answer three questionsWho are you, which funder, what do you need right now. Every one can be skipped.
  6. They get one suggestionNot a menu. One tool, with a Start button, chosen from their answers.
  7. They get a real resultA score, the comments behind it, and what to fix first. This is the moment they decide whether to come back.
Step seven is the one that matters

Everything before it is persuasion. Step seven is the product actually proving itself. That is why the prototype now includes the inside of a tool, with a sample you can run without using your own work.

6

The university path

Separate route, different tone. More about results and money, less about encouragement. It ends in a conversation, not a signup.

  1. A dean or department head arrivesOften because one of their researchers mentioned it.
  2. They read the problem in their termsFunding is harder to win and your office cannot mentor everyone one to one.
  3. They see what they would getBetter applications across the faculty, and a view of which grants are coming.
  4. They see what is not built yetThe forecasting idea is clearly marked as coming. Saying so is what keeps their trust.
  5. They ask for a demoFive fields. The reply names a real person and a real time.
7

Rules we follow everywhere

These come straight from her answers. They apply to every page and every sentence.

Be honest

Every stage says whether it is ready, half ready, or coming. Nothing unbuilt is shown as finished.

Plain words

Short sentences. No hype. Terms like study section get explained the first time.

Everyone can use it

Works on phones, on slow internet, with a keyboard, and with a screen reader. This is a requirement, not a nice extra.

Never oversell

We never say EQUITAS writes your grant. It helps you understand your own work. For NIH that difference is serious.

Lead with the person

The credibility comes from a real, working NIH reviewer. The technology is how that reaches everyone, not the headline.

Keep her brand

Same navy, gold and cream. Same fonts. The structure changes, the identity does not.

What we still need from her

Six things, and the first one blocks the most work.

  • Her answers to the four decisions, especially the sixth stage.
  • Her name, photo and permission to say she is an active NIH reviewer. This is the strongest trust point and it is missing from the site today.
  • Prices and plan limits.
  • Real screenshots of the simulator and the university views.
  • Brand files — logo, the colour file, and the fonts.
  • A decision on Arial. She chose Apple and Linear as references. Arial is the one thing working against that look, and she said fonts were open to discussion.
